When you change the nameservers or DNS records for a `.at` domain, the update does not take effect everywhere at once. Several distinct steps happen in sequence, each with its own timing. Understanding these steps helps you know when to expect your changes to be live and how to verify them properly.

## Nameserver Changes vs. DNS Record Changes

It is important to distinguish between two types of change:

- **Nameserver delegation change** — when you update which nameservers are responsible for your `.at` domain. This change is recorded in the `.at` zone at nic.at, the Austrian domain registry.
- **DNS record change** — when you update an A, AAAA, MX, CNAME, or similar record within your existing DNS zone. This happens directly on your authoritative nameservers (e.g. `ns1.edis.global` and `ns2.edis.global`) and does not require nic.at to publish anything.

<Note>
  The `.at` nameservers operated by nic.at are not root nameservers for the entire DNS — they are authoritative only for the `.at` top-level domain zone. Changes to individual DNS records within your zone (A, MX, etc.) do not go through nic.at at all, provided your nameservers remain the same.
</Note>

## The .at Zone Reload Schedule

According to the EDIS domain FAQ, nic.at updates the `.at` zone **once per hour, at the top of each hour**. This means that after you change your nameservers through your registrar:

1. The registrar sends the update to nic.at.
2. nic.at includes the change in its next hourly zone reload.
3. The new delegation becomes visible via nic.at's authoritative servers.

However, this hourly reload is only one part of the process. Even after nic.at has published the update, resolvers around the world that have cached the old delegation will continue to use the old nameservers until their cached TTL expires.

## TTL and Its Effect on Propagation

Every DNS record has a **Time to Live (TTL)** value — a number of seconds that tells resolvers how long they may cache that record before fetching a fresh copy. Common TTL values are:

| TTL Value | Cache Duration |
|---|---|
| 300 | 5 minutes |
| 3600 | 1 hour |
| 86400 | 24 hours |

If your domain's NS records had a TTL of 86400 before you changed them, resolvers that cached the old records can keep using them for up to 24 hours after the nic.at zone was updated. This is why DNS changes sometimes appear to "work for some people but not others" — it depends on whether a given resolver has expired its cache yet.

<Tip>
  If you are planning a nameserver change or a major DNS update, lower the TTL of the affected records to **300 seconds (5 minutes)** at least 24–48 hours in advance. This way, caches expire quickly after you make the actual change, and the new values propagate within minutes rather than hours.
</Tip>

## Checking That Your Changes Have Propagated

Do not rely on a single DNS lookup tool or a single network connection to confirm propagation — different resolvers may still be serving cached values.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Query the Authoritative Nameservers Directly">
    Use a DNS tool to query `ns1.edis.global` or `ns2.edis.global` directly for your domain. An authoritative answer from these servers confirms the change is live at the source, regardless of what any caching resolver returns.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Check WHOIS for Nameserver Delegation">
    Look up your domain in WHOIS (e.g. at [whois.nic.at](https://www.nic.at/en/whois-domain-search)) to see the delegated nameservers. An updated WHOIS record means nic.at has published the change — but downstream resolvers may still be serving cached answers.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Test from Multiple Networks">
    Access your website and send a test email from different internet connections (for example, mobile data and a home broadband connection) to confirm that propagation is complete from multiple vantage points.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Test Website, HTTPS, and Email Separately">
    Each service depends on different DNS records (A/AAAA for the website, MX for email). Test each one independently. A working website does not guarantee that email is also routing correctly.

    <Note>
      If you use DNSSEC, verify that the DS record, the zone signature, and the delegation are all consistent after a nameserver change. A DNSSEC validation failure will make the domain unreachable even if the basic DNS records are correct.
    </Note>
  </Step>
</Steps>
